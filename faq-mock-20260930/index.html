About Public Adjusters

1 What Does a Public Adjuster Do?

A licensed public adjuster represents you — the policyholder — on a property insurance claim. Typical work includes inspecting and documenting damage, preparing a supported estimate package, explaining coverage issues in plain language, and communicating with the carrier in claim language so you are less likely to misspeak on the record. United Claims Specialists follows inspect → respond → recover.

2 How Is a Public Adjuster Different From the Insurance Company’s Adjuster?

A staff adjuster works for the insurer. An independent adjuster is typically hired by the insurer or a third-party administrator. A public adjuster is licensed to represent you on the property claim. Same loss file, different side of the table. UCS is on your side of the file, not the carrier’s.

3 How Is a Public Adjuster Different From My Insurance Agent or Broker?

Your agent or broker helps place and service the policy — shopping coverage, explaining forms, and often taking the first notice of loss. A public adjuster advocates on the claim file itself: scope, documentation, and settlement communication with the carrier. Agents are essential partners; claim advocacy is a different licensed job. When to Hire

4 questions
5 When Does Hiring a Public Adjuster Usually Make Sense?

Hiring often helps when the loss is large or complex, the first offer feels thin compared with real repair estimates, the claim looks underpaid or incomplete for the same date of loss, you don’t have the hours to manage documentation and calls, or you want claim advocacy without jumping straight to litigation. Small, clear losses where a trusted local bid already matches the carrier number can be fine to handle yourself — honesty over pressure.

Not sure? Free consult →
6 Should I Accept the Insurance Company’s First Offer?

Often, a first offer is a starting scope after a short inspection — not a complete repair budget. Missed damage, depreciation gaps, and contractor divergences are common. Pausing for a second look before you treat the number as final can matter, because accepting can make later scope changes harder. Accepting can still be reasonable when the number already matches a trusted complete bid and you understand what’s included. This is not DIY carrier-letter coaching.

Compare a first offer with UCS →
7 Can My Contractor Negotiate the Insurance Claim for Me?

In most states, a contractor who is not separately licensed as a public adjuster should not negotiate the settlement of your insurance claim with the carrier on your behalf for compensation. Contractors are essential for scopes, repair bids, and documenting physical damage — claim advocacy is a different licensed job. UCS works alongside good contractors without blurring those roles.

8 What If My Claim Was Underpaid or the First Scope Missed Damage?

Reopening or supplementing often makes sense when there is underpayment, a thin first scope, missed or hidden damage on the same loss, or contractor bids that materially diverge from the carrier sheet. Property claims and policies are nuanced and complex — homeowner reason-code homework or self-written carrier letters can misspeak on the record. A licensed public adjuster should re-inspect, document, prepare a supported supplement package, and handle carrier communication within license rules.

11 What Is the Appraisal Clause — at a High Level?

Many property policies include an appraisal provision: a contractual process for resolving disagreements about the amount of loss (not every coverage dispute). It is separate from litigation and has its own notice, umpire, and timeline rules that vary by policy and state. Misusing appraisal — or treating it as a DIY letter campaign — can create problems on the record. United Claims Specialists can explain whether appraisal is even in play on your file during a free consult. We do not publish DIY appraisal demand templates.

Ask if appraisal applies →
12 What Are Additional Living Expenses (ALE)?

Additional living expenses (sometimes called loss-of-use) are benefits that may help cover reasonable extra costs when a covered loss makes your home uninhabitable — temporary housing, extra food, or similar necessities, subject to your policy’s limits and conditions. What qualifies, how long benefits run, and how to document receipts are policy-specific. Homeowners often under-document ALE or misspeak about “uninhabitable.” A licensed public adjuster can help align documentation with the claim file — high-level guidance, not DIY coaching.

Ask about ALE on your file →
13 What Does “Matching” Mean for Undamaged Property?

After a partial loss, repairs may leave visible mismatches — roofing, siding, flooring, or cabinetry that no longer blend with undamaged areas. Some policies and state rules address matching (paying to create a reasonably uniform appearance); others are narrower. Matching disputes are technical and easy to misspeak about. UCS reviews the declarations, endorsements, and local practice with you rather than handing you a DIY matching letter.

Storms & Deductibles

2 questions
14 What’s the Difference Between a Regular Storm Deductible and a Hurricane Deductible?

Many homeowners policies use a flat all-other-perils (AOP) deductible for everyday wind/storm claims (often a fixed dollar amount). A hurricane deductible is often a percentage of dwelling (Coverage A) limit — so the same physical damage can mean a much larger out-of-pocket if hurricane rules apply. Triggers and percentages vary by policy and state; Florida has specific timing windows tied to official hurricane activity. Wrong deductible assumptions skew settlement math. UCS reads the declarations page and claim file with you — we do not hand you a DIY “which deductible applies” worksheet. See also Storm Damage and Hurricane Damage.

15 Are There Time Limits on Property Insurance Claims?

Yes — policies and state law set notice, suit-limitation, and sometimes proof-of-loss timing that can affect options. Exact deadlines vary by state and policy wording. Waiting can shrink options; rushing into self-written carrier letters can also hurt. This FAQ is educational, not legal advice.
