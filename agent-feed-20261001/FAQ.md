## About public adjusters

### 1. What Does a Public Adjuster Do?

A licensed public adjuster represents you — the policyholder — on a property insurance claim. Typical work includes inspecting and documenting damage, preparing a supported estimate package, explaining coverage issues in plain language, and communicating with the carrier in claim language on your behalf. The real advantage is experience: someone in your corner who has already guided thousands of losses like yours through negotiation — decades of that work, so you are not carrying the file alone.

Soft next step: [Insurance Claim Help](https://www.ucspa.com/insurance-claim-help)

### 2. How Is a Public Adjuster Different From the Insurance Company’s Adjuster?

A staff adjuster works for the insurer. An independent adjuster is typically hired by the insurer or a third-party administrator. A public adjuster is licensed to represent you on the property claim. Same loss file, different side of the table. UCS is on your side of the file, not the carrier’s.

### 3. How Is a Public Adjuster Different From My Insurance Agent or Broker?

Your agent or broker helps place and service the policy — shopping coverage, explaining forms, and often taking the first notice of loss. A public adjuster advocates on the claim file itself: scope, documentation, and settlement communication with the carrier. Agents are essential partners; claim advocacy is a different licensed job.

### 4. Does UCS Handle Commercial Property Claims as Well as Residential?

Yes. Alongside homes and condos, United Claims Specialists brings vast commercial experience — shopping plazas, restaurants, apartments, nursing homes, bowling alleys, industrial parks, and full community claims of 80+ buildings. Those scopes often need tighter documentation, clearer coverage conversations, and steadier carrier communication than a typical residential file.

## When to hire

### 5. When Does Hiring a Public Adjuster Usually Make Sense?

Hiring often helps when the loss is large or complex, the first offer feels thin compared with real repair estimates, the claim looks underpaid or incomplete for the same date of loss, you don’t have the hours to manage documentation and calls, or you want claim advocacy without jumping straight to litigation. On those files, having someone in your corner with decades of experience and thousands of losses like yours successfully negotiated matters — carrying that alone is a heavy lift. Small, clear losses where a trusted local bid already matches the carrier number can still move forward without counsel.

### 6. Is It Best to Involve a Public Adjuster From Claim Onset?

Often, yes — especially on larger or complex losses. The earliest steps of a claim set the record: how coverage is applied, which limitations are assumed, and how the loss is described when it is filed. Early missteps — improper coverage application, incorrect limitation assumptions, or incomplete / improper filing — can lead to underpayment, a thin or incomplete scope, statements that are hard to unwind later, and in some cases a coverage dispute. Property claims are nuanced; United Claims Specialists recommends a licensed public adjuster from the start so documentation and carrier communication are handled carefully.

### 7. Should I Accept the Insurance Company’s First Offer?

Often, a first offer is a starting scope after a short inspection — not a complete repair budget. Missed damage, depreciation gaps, and contractor divergences are common. Pausing for a second look before you treat the number as final can matter, because accepting can make later scope changes harder. Accepting can still be reasonable when the number already matches a trusted complete bid and you understand what’s included. A licensed public adjuster can compare the offer to a supported estimate — start at [Insurance Claim Help](https://www.ucspa.com/insurance-claim-help) or call (855) 321-5677.

### 8. Can My Contractor Negotiate the Insurance Claim for Me?

In most states, a contractor who is not separately licensed as a public adjuster negotiating your insurance claim settlement with the carrier for compensation is actually illegal — and it can amount to the unlicensed practice of public adjusting (UPPA). This is not legal advice; rules vary by state. Good contractors remain essential for scopes, repair bids, and documenting physical damage — claim advocacy is a different licensed job. UCS works alongside good contractors without blurring those roles.

### 9. What If My Claim Was Underpaid or the First Scope Missed Damage?

Reopening or supplementing often makes sense when there is underpayment, a thin first scope, missed or hidden damage on the same loss, or contractor bids that materially diverge from the carrier sheet. Property claims and policies are nuanced and complex. A licensed public adjuster should re-inspect, document, prepare a supported supplement package, and handle carrier communication within license rules — the same craft that comes from decades of files like yours.

### 12. What Is the Appraisal Clause?

Many property policies include an appraisal provision: a contractual process for resolving disagreements about the amount of loss (not every coverage dispute). It is separate from litigation and has its own notice, umpire, and timeline rules that vary by policy and state. Appraisal has notice and timeline rules worth getting right. United Claims Specialists can explain whether appraisal is even in play on your file during a free consult, and help document the amount of loss if it is.

### 13. What Are Additional Living Expenses (ALE)?

Additional living expenses (sometimes called loss-of-use) are benefits that may help cover reasonable extra costs when a covered loss makes your home uninhabitable — temporary housing, extra food, or similar necessities, subject to your policy’s limits and conditions. What qualifies, how long benefits run, and how to document receipts are policy-specific. A licensed public adjuster can help align documentation with the claim file.

### 14. What Does “Matching” Mean for Undamaged Property?

After a partial loss, repairs may leave visible mismatches — roofing, siding, flooring, or cabinetry that no longer blend with undamaged areas. Some policies and state rules address matching (paying to create a reasonably uniform appearance); others are narrower. Matching disputes are technical. UCS reviews the declarations, endorsements, and local practice with you so documentation and carrier communication stay accurate.

## Storms & deductibles

### 15. What’s the Difference Between a Regular Storm Deductible and a Hurricane Deductible?

Many homeowners policies use a flat all-other-perils (AOP) deductible for everyday wind/storm claims (often a fixed dollar amount). A hurricane deductible is often a percentage of dwelling (Coverage A) limit — so the same physical damage can mean a much larger out-of-pocket if hurricane rules apply. Triggers and percentages vary by policy and state; Florida has specific timing windows tied to official hurricane activity. Wrong deductible assumptions skew settlement math. UCS reads the declarations page and claim file with you so settlement math starts from the right deductible. See also [Storm Damage](https://www.ucspa.com/storm-damage) and [Hurricane Damage](https://www.ucspa.com/hurricane-damage).

### 16. Are There Time Limits on Property Insurance Claims?

Yes — policies and state law set notice, suit-limitation, and sometimes proof-of-loss timing that can affect options. Exact deadlines vary by state and policy wording. Waiting can shrink options; rushing without clear documentation can also hurt. A licensed public adjuster can help you understand where your file sits and what documentation still helps.
